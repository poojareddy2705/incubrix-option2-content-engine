From One Interview to a Full Week of Content

If you've ever sat down after recording an interview or webinar and thought, "I know there's good stuff in there — I just don't know how to get it out," you're not alone. The gap between a great recorded conversation and a week of consistent social content isn't talent. It's process.

Here's the process I've landed on, and why each step exists.

Start with the transcript, not the video.

This sounds obvious once you've tried it, but most people go back to the video first — scrubbing through the timeline, pausing and rewinding, trying to remember what felt significant while they were recording it. That's slow and exhausting. Text is scannable in a way that video isn't. When you have a full transcript in front of you, you can read it in a fraction of the time it takes to watch the recording, and your eye naturally catches the lines that stand out. Start there.

Identify your top three to five moments.

You're not looking for a summary of everything discussed. You're looking for the moments worth isolating — the specific insight that reframes a common assumption, the story that makes an abstract concept suddenly concrete, the moment of honest vulnerability that makes the guest or speaker feel real. Most hour-long interviews have maybe four or five of these. If you're finding more than that, you're probably including moments that are good in context but weak on their own.

Score each clip before you commit to it.

Not all good moments make good clips. I think about three things when I'm deciding which ones to actually repurpose. First: hook strength. Does the opening sentence stop someone, or does it ease them in? Easing in is fine in long-form; it's death in short-form. Second: emotional resonance. Does this make the viewer feel something — recognition, curiosity, surprise, relief? Content that generates an emotion generates engagement. Third: novelty. Is this something the audience has heard before in roughly this form, or does it offer a genuinely different angle? Familiar topics can work, but they need a fresh frame.

Adapt each clip for the platform it's going to.

The same insight requires a completely different treatment depending on where it's landing. For LinkedIn, the educational angle wins — lead with the takeaway, give the context, end with a question that invites a professional response. For Instagram, you're competing visually and emotionally; the hook has to be visceral and the caption should be short enough to read before someone loses interest. For YouTube Shorts, you have about two seconds before someone swipes. The very first frame and the very first word have to earn attention immediately, with no warm-up.

Build in a human review step for AI-generated copy.

If you're using AI to draft hooks or captions, treat the output as a first draft, not a final one. Read it against two criteria: does it sound like you, and does it accurately represent what was actually said? AI tends toward language that's slightly too polished, slightly too generic. It smooths out the texture that makes a creator's voice recognizable. Your job in review is to put that texture back.

Publish consistently, not constantly.

The biggest mistake I see creators make with repurposed content is trying to post everything at once when they finally get organized, then going quiet for two weeks. Consistency matters more than volume. A predictable publishing cadence — even if it's modest — builds audience expectation and trust in a way that sporadic high-volume posting never does. Spread one interview across five or six days. Let each piece breathe.

One recorded conversation, done right, is more than enough for a full week of meaningful content.
